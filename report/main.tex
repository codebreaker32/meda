% ================================================================= ABSTRACT
\section*{Abstract}\label{sec:abstract}
Micro-electrode-dot-array (MEDA) biochips move nanolitre droplets over a grid
of micro-electrode cells (MCs) whose electrodes degrade with use, so a droplet
may fail to move where the routing policy expects. Elfar et al.\ \cite{elfar2023}
route droplets reliably with deep reinforcement learning: a convolutional
neural network (CNN) reads the sensed MC health together with the droplet and
goal locations as a three-channel image, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
learns the routing policy. This project first reproduces that CNN--PPO method as a tested,
open simulation and training framework, validated against the authors'
own trained agent. It then investigates a topology-aware alternative in
which every MC is a graph node connected to its eight neighbours, a Graph
Neural Network (GNN) encodes the graph, and global max pooling produces the
state representation for the same PPO learner. The report defines the
problem, the reproduced baseline, the proposed graph methodology, the
simulation platform and the evaluation protocol, and reports preliminary
results from a reduced ($16\times16$, single-seed) experiment.

% ================================================================= 1 INTRODUCTION
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{Digital microfluidics and MEDA biochips.}
Digital microfluidic biochips automate biochemical protocols by manipulating
discrete droplets on a planar array of electrodes using
electrowetting-on-dielectric (EWOD) \cite{pollack2000,chakrabarty2006}.
The micro-electrode-dot-array (MEDA) architecture refines this idea: the
chip consists of thousands of identical micro-electrode cells (MCs), each with
its own control and sensing circuitry \cite{wang2011,li2017}. A droplet covers
a rectangle of several MCs, and the controller moves it by actuating the MCs at
its leading edge. Because each MC can also sense its own state, the controller
has real-time information about the chip.

\paragraph{Droplet routing.}
A bioassay is executed as a sequence of fluidic operations (dispensing, mixing,
splitting, detection), and between them droplets are transported across the
chip. Each such routing job moves a droplet of a given size from a start
location to a goal location inside an allowed routing zone. At every control
cycle, the routing policy chooses the direction in which the droplet is moved.

\paragraph{Reliability and MC degradation.}
Repeated actuation causes charge trapping in the dielectric, so an MC's EWOD
force decreases with the number of times it has been actuated. A droplet
whose leading edge lies over degraded MCs may move more slowly than planned or
not at all \cite{elfar2023}. Routing therefore cannot simply follow the
geometrically shortest path: it must take the current health of the MCs into
account, and adapt as the chip ages.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning and the base paper.}
Elfar et al.\ \cite{elfar2022date,elfar2023} formulate adaptive routing as a
sequential decision problem and solve it with deep reinforcement learning.
The agent observes a three-channel grid (MC health, droplet location, goal
location); a CNN encodes it; and a PPO actor--critic \cite{schulman2017}
chooses one of eight movement directions. Two roles must be kept apart:
the \emph{CNN is the state encoder}, while \emph{PPO is the learning
algorithm}, with an actor (policy) and a critic (value estimate).

\paragraph{This project.}
We reproduce the CNN--PPO method, the \emph{baseline}, and investigate
replacing only its state encoder with a GNN on an explicit MC graph. PPO,
the simulator, the reward and the action space all stay the same.

% ================================================================= 2 MOTIVATION
\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

A MEDA chip is physically a set of MCs with fixed spatial neighbourhoods, and
routing decisions depend on how health values, the droplet and the goal are
arranged within those neighbourhoods. This suggests a direct graph
abstraction: each MC becomes a node, each pair of spatially adjacent MCs is
joined by an edge, and the per-MC observation values become node features.

A CNN already captures local spatial relationships through its convolution
kernels. The baseline, however, treats the chip as an image of fixed size:
every chip is resampled to a $30\times30$ observation \cite{elfar2023}, and
the fully connected layer after the convolutions accounts for 29.5 of the
29.7 million parameters of the Table~I network. A GNN instead uses the same
weights at every node. Its parameter count does not depend on the chip size,
and it can operate on the native MC grid of any chip. This project
investigates whether this explicit, topology-aware representation is an
effective state encoder for PPO-based routing. It does not presume that the
GNN is better. The central research question is:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can an explicit topology-aware graph representation provide an
effective state representation for PPO-based droplet routing on MEDA
biochips?}
\end{quote}

The graph representation is read out with \emph{global max pooling}, the
element-wise maximum over all node embeddings. It is the methodology's
initial choice because it is simple, invariant to node ordering and of fixed
size for any chip. It may, however, discard which node produced a maximum and
hence where the droplet and goal are. Its adequacy is therefore evaluated
experimentally, not assumed.

% ================================================================= 3 LITERATURE SURVEY
\section{Literature Survey}\label{sec:lit}

\paragraph{Digital microfluidics and MEDA.}
EWOD-based droplet actuation was demonstrated by Pollack et al.\ \cite{pollack2000},
and design-automation methods for digital microfluidic biochips (synthesis,
testing, reconfiguration) are surveyed by Chakrabarty and Su
\cite{chakrabarty2006}. Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2011} introduced the MEDA
architecture, whose fine-grained MCs allow droplets of different sizes and
shapes. Li et al.\ \cite{li2017} developed droplet-size-aware synthesis that
exploits this flexibility.

\paragraph{Classical and formal droplet routing.}
Early work treated droplet routing as a combinatorial problem on a fixed
electrode grid. Su et al.\ \cite{su2006} routed droplets as part of biochip
synthesis while avoiding droplet interference. These methods assume that
electrodes behave ideally. Elfar et al.\ \cite{elfar2021date,elfar2022tcad}
modelled MC degradation and stochastic droplet movement explicitly. They then
synthesized routing strategies with formal methods (stochastic games solved
with PRISM-games), which are reliable but become costly as chips and bioassays
grow.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning for routing.}
Liang et al.\ \cite{liang2020} applied deep reinforcement learning to
adaptive droplet routing on conventional digital microfluidic biochips. They
later extended it to parallel control of multiple droplets on MEDA with
multi-agent reinforcement learning \cite{liang2021}. Elfar et al.\
\cite{elfar2022date,elfar2023}, the \emph{base paper} of this project, route
droplets on MEDA biochips with a CNN--PPO agent that observes the sensed MC
health. The agent uses a parameterized action space whose step size adapts to
the droplet size, and transfer learning across chip sizes.
Their evaluation includes COVID-19 bioassays and experiments on fabricated
prototypes.

\paragraph{Graph neural networks and graph-based reinforcement learning.}
Message-passing neural networks \cite{gilmer2017} update each node's
embedding from its neighbours. The Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)
\cite{kipf2017} does so with a normalized neighbourhood average, and
GraphSAGE \cite{hamilton2017} generalizes aggregation to unseen graphs.
Relational GCNs \cite{schlichtkrull2018} learn separate weights per edge
type. Xu et al.\ \cite{xu2019} analyse how the aggregation and readout
functions bound what a GNN can distinguish, which is relevant to the choice of
global max pooling here. Combining GNN encoders with reinforcement learning
has succeeded in chip design. In macro placement, for example, a graph
encoder of the netlist feeds a policy trained with PPO \cite{mirhoseini2021}.

\paragraph{Position of this work.}
The surveyed MEDA routing methods encode the chip state either as formal
models \cite{elfar2022tcad} or as images for a CNN \cite{elfar2023,liang2021}.
None of them represents the MC array as a graph for a GNN encoder. This
project studies that representation. It keeps every other component of the
base paper fixed, so that any difference can be attributed to the state
encoder.

% ================================================================= 4 PROBLEM STATEMENT
\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Routing job and chip model.}
A $W\times H$ chip holds MCs indexed by $(x,y)$. A droplet is the rectangle
$\delta=(x_a,y_a,x_b,y_b)$ of MCs it covers. A routing job
$(\delta_s,\delta_g,\delta_h)$ asks to move the droplet from the start
$\delta_s$ to the goal $\delta_g$ within the routing (hazard) zone
$\delta_h$. Following the base paper \cite{elfar2023}, the degradation of MC
$(i,j)$ after $n_{ij}$ actuations and its sensed health are
\begin{equation}
  D_{ij}=\tau_{ij}^{\,n_{ij}/c_{ij}}, \qquad
  H_{ij}=\big\lfloor 2^{b} D_{ij}\big\rfloor ,
\end{equation}
with $\tau_{ij}\in[0.5,0.7]$ and $c_{ij}\in[500,800]$ fitted from
measurements, and a $b$-bit health sensor. A move succeeds stochastically
with a probability given by the degradation of the MCs at the droplet's
leading edge, and every actuation increases their $n_{ij}$.

\paragraph{Reinforcement-learning formulation (baseline).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{State / observation:} a three-channel $W\times H$ grid of
        MC health ($H/2^b$ inside the routing zone, 0 outside), the droplet
        location and the goal location.
  \item \textbf{Actions:} eight directions N, S, E, W, NE, NW, SE, SW. The
        step size adapts to the droplet size and never overshoots the goal
        (Algorithm~1 of \cite{elfar2023}).
  \item \textbf{Transition:} the stochastic, health-dependent movement of the
        simulator.
  \item \textbf{Reward:} $r = a_{dis}\,r_{dis} + a_{ter}\,r_{ter} + a_{act}\,r_{act}$,
        combining progress towards the goal, a terminal reward on arrival and a
        penalty for invalid actions \cite{elfar2023}.
  \item \textbf{Episode end:} the droplet reaches the goal, or $k_{\max}$
        control cycles elapse.
  \item \textbf{Learner:} PPO with an actor--critic network \cite{schulman2017}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Representation question.}
The baseline encodes the state as
\emph{3-channel grid $\rightarrow$ CNN $\rightarrow$ Flatten $\rightarrow$ PPO}.
This project studies the alternative
\emph{MC graph $\rightarrow$ GNN $\rightarrow$ global max pooling $\rightarrow$ PPO}.
The proposed graph (not part of the base paper) is
\begin{equation}
  G=(V,E,X),\qquad N=|V|=WH,\qquad X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3},
\end{equation}
where $V$ are the MC nodes, $E$ the spatial adjacencies and $X$ the three
baseline features per node. The problem is to determine whether this
representation lets PPO learn reliable and efficient routing policies, and
how it compares with the CNN--PPO baseline under identical conditions.

\section{Objectives and Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Baseline reproduction:} reproduce the CNN--PPO method of the
        base paper and validate it. (\emph{Completed.})
  \item \textbf{Graph representation:} represent each MC as a node and
        spatially adjacent MCs as edges, retaining the baseline's state
        information exactly. (\emph{Completed.})
  \item \textbf{GNN state encoding:} replace the CNN encoder with a GNN and a
        global max-pooling readout while retaining PPO. (\emph{Completed.})
  \item \textbf{Controlled evaluation:} compare both encoders under matched
        simulation, PPO, training budget and held-out evaluation jobs.
        (\emph{Preliminary run completed; full-scale runs in progress.})
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reproduced baseline (CNN--PPO)}
The simulator and training pipeline were re-implemented from the paper in
Python (Section~\ref{sec:platform}). The paper leaves several details open,
such as the reward coefficients, the health-sensor resolution and PPO settings.
These were taken from the first author's public reference code, and are
marked as such (Table~\ref{tab:params}). The CNN of Table~I in
\cite{elfar2023} has three $3\times3$ convolutions with 64, 128 and 128
filters, a 256-unit fully connected layer, and linear actor (8 action logits)
and critic ($V(s)$) heads. Every chip is observed at $30\times30$.
Training runs 8 parallel environments for $2^{14}$ steps per epoch, then
evaluates the policy on 500 random routing jobs. A dynamic learning rate starts
at $\eta_0=3.5\times10^{-4}$ and is multiplied by $0.7$ whenever the success
rate exceeds 99\% (floor $10^{-6}$).

\paragraph{Validation.}
The reproduction was checked in three ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The authors' own trained $30\times30$ agent, converted from
        TensorFlow to PyTorch and never trained on our code, routes
        \textbf{100\%} of 300 random jobs in \textbf{10.0 cycles} in our
        simulator. Its original training log reports 99.8--100\% and
        $\approx$10.5 cycles, so the movement model, actions, routing zones
        and job distribution match.
  \item In the bioassay setting of the paper (COVID-RAT and COVID-PCR on a
        $60\times30$ chip), the reproduced health-agnostic baseline finishes
        in 207 and 772 cycles on average. Both values lie within the ranges
        shown in Fig.~9 of \cite{elfar2023}.
  \item 362 automated tests cover the model, environment, training, routers
        and bioassays.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proposed methodology (GNN--PPO)}
\paragraph{Terminology.} \emph{GNN} (graph neural network) names the whole
family of networks that learn on graphs by passing messages between
neighbouring nodes. A \emph{GCN} (graph convolutional network) is one
particular GNN, defined by its message-passing rule (Step~3 below). The
proposed approach is a GNN--PPO router; the GNN used in it is a GCN, as the
methodology specifies. The \emph{direction-aware GCN} of
Section~\ref{sec:results} is another GNN, which differs from the GCN only in
giving each neighbour direction its own weights.

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} contrasts the two pipelines; only the encoder and
readout differ.

\paragraph{Step 1: graph construction.}
Each MC is one node, with the fixed index $\mathrm{node}(x,y)=y\,W+x$. This
is the row-major order of the observation tensor, so the conversion is
deterministic. Edges join every MC to its spatial neighbours in \textbf{eight
directions}: left, right, up, down and the four diagonals. This is a
supervisor-approved correction of the initial four-direction design, made for
consistency with the eight-direction action space. Edges are stored in both
directions so that messages flow both ways. For a $W\times H$ chip,
\begin{equation}
|E| = 2\big[(W-1)H + W(H-1) + 2(W-1)(H-1)\big],
\end{equation}
which gives 6\,844 directed edges on $30\times30$. Corner MCs have 3
neighbours, border MCs 5 and interior MCs 8. In this first version edges
carry \textbf{no attributes}: they indicate adjacency only. The
health-dependent movement probabilities stay inside the simulator, and a
degraded MC remains a node, carrying its low health value
(Figure~\ref{fig:graph}).

\paragraph{Step 2: node features.}
Node $i$ carries exactly the three baseline values at its MC,
$X_i=[\text{health},\text{droplet},\text{goal}]$, copied from the observation
tensor without re-normalization. The implementation checks this parity
element-wise, together with the node count, edge set, degrees and batched
shapes (``graph validation'', Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Step 3: GNN message passing.}
The encoder is a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) \cite{kipf2017} with
$L=3$ layers, hidden dimension $d=64$ and ReLU activations:
\begin{equation}
  H^{(l+1)} = \mathrm{ReLU}\!\big(\hat{D}^{-1/2}\hat{A}\hat{D}^{-1/2} H^{(l)} W^{(l)} + b^{(l)}\big),
  \qquad \hat{A}=A+I,\quad H^{(0)}=X,
\end{equation}
producing node embeddings $Z=H^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$. Aggregation is
implemented as a sparse matrix product over the explicit edge list, so it
runs on CPU and GPU without an extra graph library. The number of layers and
the hidden size are our assumptions, since the methodology leaves them open.

\paragraph{Step 4: global max pooling.}
The graph embedding is the element-wise maximum over nodes,
\begin{equation}
  g_k=\max_{i\in V} Z_{i,k}, \qquad g\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
\end{equation}
a fixed-size vector for any chip. Whether it retains enough information about
droplet position, goal position and MC health is evaluated experimentally.

\paragraph{Step 5: PPO actor--critic.}
As in the baseline, linear heads on $g$ give the actor's probabilities over
the same eight actions and the critic's value $V(s)$. PPO, its settings and
the training schedule are unchanged. The GNN encoder has 8.6\,k parameters,
against 29.7\,M for the Table~I CNN.

\subsection{Evaluation plan}
Both methods are trained with identical configuration files. The GNN
configuration inherits every baseline setting and changes only the encoder;
an automated test enforces this. Both are then evaluated on the same held-out
routing jobs, whose random seed differs from the training and model-selection
seeds. The metrics are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item success rate and failure rate;
  \item mean, median and standard deviation of routing cycles, with timeouts
        counted at $k_{\max}$;
  \item invalid-action rate;

\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Requirements.}
The package installs with \texttt{pip install -e .} and needs Python
$\geq3.9$ and PyTorch $\geq2.0$. Each command uses the best available device:
a CUDA GPU (the one with the most free memory), else Apple MPS, else the CPU.
The PyTorch build must match the server's GPU driver, here CUDA 12.6. A
script runs any experiment on a GPU server of the local network over SSH.
The paper-size CNN trains at about 20\,s per epoch on the A100.
On the 4-core CPU the same network is estimated at roughly 35\,min per epoch,
so all full-scale runs use the server.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Every run stores in its own folder:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the exact configuration and random seed;
  \item the latest and best models, plus periodic checkpoints;
  \item a per-epoch log of environment steps, optimizer updates, success
        rate and routing cycles;
  \item training-curve plots.
\end{itemize}
The comparison tool writes tables of settings, per-job results and summary
metrics, and every parameter is tagged with its source. The code, configuration
files and results are version-controlled, and continuous integration runs the
test suite on Python 3.10 and 3.12.

\section{Preliminary Results}\label{sec:results}

\emph{These are preliminary results from a reduced experiment, reported
with their configuration. They are not final conclusions.}

\paragraph{Graph validation.}
All 14 checks pass on a $30\times30$ observation: node count $=W\!H=900$,
feature dimension 3, and exactly 6\,844 directed 8-neighbour edges stored in
both directions without duplicates. Corner and interior degrees are 3 and 8,
the node index satisfies $\mathrm{node}(x,y)=yW+x$, the health, droplet and
goal features equal the grid values exactly, and batched tensor shapes are
correct.

\paragraph{Controlled comparison.}
Setup:
\begin{itemize}
  \item healthy $16\times16$ chips at native resolution;
  \item one seed and 327\,680 environment steps per method, with identical
        PPO settings;
  \item evaluation on the same 500 held-out jobs.
\end{itemize}
The $16\times16$ CNN baseline uses the smaller CNN of the reference code
(2.15\,M parameters) to fit a CPU. Table~\ref{tab:results} and
Figure~\ref{fig:curves} give the results.

\paragraph{Observations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The GCN with global max pooling, as initially specified, did
        not learn to route in this run.} 88\% of its decisions are invalid
        moves.
  \item \textbf{The GCN layer is the cause, not max pooling.} The GCN averages
        all neighbours alike, whatever their direction. A job and its mirror
        image (goal to the east vs.\ goal to the west) therefore produce
        identical embeddings, and hence the same action probabilities, even
        though their correct moves are opposite. An automated test confirms
        this symmetry.
  \item \textbf{Direction-aware message passing removes the symmetry.} A
        relational GCN \cite{schlichtkrull2018} gives each of the eight
        neighbour directions its own weights, while still storing no edge
        attributes. Changing only this layer raised success to 99.4\% at
        4.89 cycles in this run.
  \item \textbf{Max pooling is sufficient once the layers encode direction.}
        A role-aware readout, which adds the mean embeddings of the droplet
        and goal nodes, did not help the isotropic GCN (5.0\%).
\end{itemize}
These observations come from a single seed on a reduced problem. They will
be tested with five seeds on $30\times30$ chips before any conclusion is
drawn.

% ================================================================= 8 CONCLUSION
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

This project addresses reliable droplet routing on MEDA biochips, where
electrode degradation makes droplet movement stochastic and routing must use
the sensed health of the micro-electrodes.

\paragraph{Completed.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item the literature study;
  \item a complete, tested reimplementation of the CNN--PPO base paper,
        validated against the authors' trained agent and the paper's bioassay
        results;
  \item the graph representation and GNN--PPO pipeline, with automated
        validation;
  \item a reproducible experiment framework that runs on CPU or GPU;
  \item a first controlled comparison and two ablations.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Proposed methodology.}
The proposed methodology represents the chip as a topology-aware graph. MCs
are nodes, the eight spatial adjacencies are edges, and the baseline's three
features are node features. A GNN with global max pooling replaces the CNN
encoder, while PPO and the rest of the setup remain unchanged. The
preliminary results suggest that the message-passing layer must be sensitive
to direction.

\paragraph{Future work.}
The remaining steps are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item run the controlled comparison at the paper's scale ($30\times30$,
        five seeds, including both ablations) on the GPU server;
  \item evaluate routing success, routing cycles, convergence and
        computational cost against the CNN--PPO baseline;
  \item study generalization across chip sizes, which the GNN's
        size-independent weights allow without resizing observations;
  \item test chips with injected faults and the COVID-19 bioassay benchmarks.
\end{itemize}
